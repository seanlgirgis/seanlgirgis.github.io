Foundations

Data architecture is the blueprint for how data is collected, stored, transformed, governed, secured, and consumed across an organization. It connects business trust with technical design: dashboards, compliance, machine learning, and operational reporting all depend on architectural decisions made upstream.

A senior Data Engineer should explain architecture as a system of tradeoffs, not a list of tools. The important questions are where data originates, how it moves, who owns it, how quality is enforced, how access is controlled, and how downstream teams consume trusted datasets.

Architecture interviews test systems thinking. A strong answer explains reliability, governance, scalability, cost, and failure recovery together.
Workload Purpose Typical Systems
OLTP Operational transactions with low latency PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, DynamoDB
OLAP Analytics, reporting, aggregations, historical scans Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, Databricks
Do not run heavy analytics directly on production OLTP systems. Large scans can create contention, increase latency, and destabilize customer-facing applications.

Core Architecture Patterns

Warehouses, lakes, and lakehouses solve different problems. A warehouse prioritizes curated SQL analytics and governance. A lake prioritizes flexible, low-cost storage for raw and semi-structured data. A lakehouse combines lake storage with table formats, transactions, and stronger metadata management.

Pattern Best For Tradeoff
Warehouse BI, structured reporting, governed SQL analytics Higher cost and less raw-data flexibility
Data Lake Raw storage, ML, logs, large-scale ingestion Can become a data swamp without standards
Lakehouse Unified analytics and ML on open storage Metadata and operational complexity

Batch, Streaming, Lambda, and Kappa

Batch processing is simpler, cheaper, and easier to debug. Streaming provides low latency but introduces event ordering, late arrivals, state management, replay, and backpressure. Lambda architecture uses both batch and speed layers; Kappa architecture simplifies the model by treating the stream as the source of truth.

Streaming is not automatically better. If the business can accept hourly or daily freshness, batch may be the stronger architecture choice.
Lambda can duplicate business logic across batch and streaming paths. Kappa reduces duplication, but replay and state recovery still require careful design.

Modeling and Storage

Analytics modeling starts with access patterns. Fact tables store measurable business events such as orders, clicks, payments, or shipments. Dimension tables add descriptive context such as customer, product, geography, and calendar attributes.

Star schemas are usually preferred for analytics because they keep queries simple and performant. Snowflake schemas reduce redundancy by normalizing dimensions, but they add joins and increase query complexity.

Concept Role Interview Angle
Fact table Measurable events and metrics Grain must be explicit
Dimension table Business context and attributes Supports slicing and filtering
Star schema Fact directly joined to dimensions Simple and BI-friendly
Snowflake schema Normalized dimensions Less duplication, more joins 
-- Example analytics grain:
-- One row per order line item.
fact_order_line_items
  order_id
  line_item_id
  customer_id
  product_id
  order_timestamp
  quantity
  gross_revenue
  discount_amount
Weak models hide grain. If the table grain is unclear, metrics will drift across dashboards and teams will calculate the same KPI differently.

Governance, Quality, and Security

Governance defines ownership, discoverability, compliance, and auditability. Metadata catalogs, lineage systems, and data contracts help teams understand where data comes from, who owns it, and what will break if a schema or metric changes.

Quality and observability make data systems operationally trustworthy. Strong pipelines monitor freshness, completeness, uniqueness, schema drift, volume anomalies, and distribution changes. A successful job is not enough; the data must be correct and usable.

Data contracts turn implicit producer-consumer expectations into explicit agreements: schema, semantics, ownership, freshness, and quality rules.
Area Production Control
Access Least privilege, role-based access, short-lived credentials
Encryption KMS or managed encryption at rest; TLS in transit
PII Masking, tokenization, row-level security, audit logs
Lineage Impact analysis and debugging across upstream and downstream assets
Governance is not paperwork. Without ownership, lineage, and quality boundaries, a technically impressive platform can still lose business trust.

Performance and Cost

Performance and cost are connected. Columnar formats such as Parquet reduce I/O by reading only required columns. Partitioning reduces scan volume. Clustering improves pruning. Materialized views speed repeated aggregations. Compaction prevents query engines from wasting time opening thousands of tiny files.

Pattern Benefit Risk
Partitioning Reduces scanned data Over-partitioning creates metadata overhead
Parquet Column pruning and compression Requires proper file sizing
Compaction Fixes small-file overhead Extra maintenance jobs
Materialized views Faster dashboards Refresh cost and staleness
Senior answers connect tuning to economics: fewer scans, fewer tiny files, better pruning, fewer duplicate pipelines, and clear retention policies.
The small-file problem is one of the most common lakehouse performance failures. Frequent micro-batches and excessive partitions can make metadata operations dominate actual processing.

System Design Walkthrough

Scenario: design a scalable analytics platform for an e-commerce company with millions of daily events, real-time dashboards, historical analytics, ML training support, governance, and compliance requirements.

A strong design starts with ingestion. Use Kafka or Kinesis for event streams and CDC for operational database changes. Land immutable raw data into object storage as bronze. Transform and validate data into silver with Spark, Flink, or dbt-style workflows. Publish gold tables for dashboards, KPIs, and ML features.

Do not design only the happy path. Explain what happens when upstream schemas change, events arrive late, jobs retry, or downstream dashboards show bad numbers.

Interview Q&A

Q: Why not run analytics directly on OLTP databases?
A: OLTP systems are optimized for low-latency transactions, not large analytical scans. Heavy reporting queries can create contention and slow production workloads. Separating OLTP from OLAP improves reliability, scalability, and workload isolation.
Q: How do you choose between a data warehouse, lake, and lakehouse?
A: Use a warehouse when governed SQL analytics is the primary need. Use a lake for low-cost raw storage and flexible ingestion. Use a lakehouse when you need open storage, ACID table behavior, ML support, and warehouse-like reliability.
Q: When should batch be preferred over streaming?
A: Choose batch when the business does not require real-time decisions. Batch is simpler to debug, cheaper to operate, and easier to reason about. Streaming is valuable when low latency justifies the added complexity.
Q: What is the value of medallion architecture?
A: It creates clear quality boundaries between raw, validated, and business-ready data. Bronze supports replay and audit history, silver applies validation and standardization, and gold serves trusted consumption. This structure improves debugging and ownership.
Q: Why is idempotency important in data pipelines?
A: Distributed systems fail and retry constantly. Idempotent processing ensures retries or replays do not create duplicate or corrupted results. This is essential for reliable CDC, streaming, backfills, and recovery workflows.
Q: What separates a senior architecture answer from a junior answer?
A: Junior answers focus on definitions and tools. Senior answers explain tradeoffs, failure modes, cost, governance, data quality, and operational ownership. Interviewers want to know how the design behaves under pressure.

Cheat Sheet

OLTP
Transactional systems optimized for small writes, consistency, and low latency.
OLAP
Analytical systems optimized for scans, joins, aggregations, and historical queries.
Warehouse
Curated structured analytics platform with strong SQL and governance.
Lake
Low-cost raw storage for structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data.
Lakehouse
Lake storage plus ACID tables, metadata, governance, and analytics reliability.
Batch
Interval-based processing; simpler and cheaper when real time is not required.
Streaming
Continuous event processing with low latency and higher operational complexity.
Bronze
Raw immutable ingestion layer used for audit, replay, and recovery.
Silver
Validated and standardized layer with deduplication and quality rules.
Gold
Business-ready tables for dashboards, KPIs, data products, and ML features.
Fact
Measurable event table such as orders, clicks, payments, or shipments.
Dimension
Descriptive context table such as customer, product, calendar, or geography.
Lineage
Tracks where data came from and what downstream assets depend on it.
Contract
Producer-consumer agreement covering schema, semantics, freshness, and ownership.
Idempotency
Retry-safe processing that avoids duplicates and corrupted downstream results.
Small files
Too many tiny files hurt metadata handling, planning time, and query performance.
